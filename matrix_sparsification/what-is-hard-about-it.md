# The problem, what is proved hard about it, and why the exact method here is not a contradiction

> Given `U` over a field `F`, find invertible `V` minimising `nnz(U V)`.

Equivalently: **choose the basis of `U`'s column space with the fewest nonzeros**,
since the columns of `U V` are a basis of that space and every basis arises from
some invertible `V`. `nnz + nns` is the same problem under a different weight.

## It is four problems with four names

The inner question — *the lightest nonzero vector in a subspace* — is one object
the literature names four ways, and they are the same:

| name | field | where |
|---|---|---|
| **minimum distance** of a linear code | any `F_q` | coding theory |
| **sparsest vector in a subspace** | `R`, `Q` | compressed sensing, `[qu2020]` |
| **spark** of a matrix | `Q` | `[tillmann2014]` |
| **girth**, or the shortest circuit of a represented matroid | any | matroid theory |

`[bhattacharyya2021]` states the identification outright: the minimum distance of
the code generated by `A` is the smallest linearly dependent set among the columns
of `A^⊥`, which is the shortest circuit of a binary matroid. **Our greedy's first
call is exactly this problem**, so everything below applies to it.

## Hard over `Q`, hard over `F_q`, and the two proofs are unrelated

**Over `Q` and `R`.** `[mccormick1983]` proves the sparsest-basis problem NP-hard;
`[colemanpothen1986]` the sparse null space basis problem; `[gottlieb2010]` that it
cannot be approximated within `2^(log^(0.5−o(1)) n)`, which is the result
`[beniamini2020]` builds its oracles around. `[tillmann2014]` sharpens the inner
problem: deciding whether a rational matrix has a circuit of size `≤ k` is
**strongly** NP-complete.

**Over a finite field.** `[vardy1997]` closes the minimum distance problem as
NP-complete over `GF(2)`. `[dumer2003]` makes it inapproximable within any
constant, and within `2^(log^(1−ε) n)`, **over every finite field**, under
randomised reductions; `[cheng2012]`, `[austrin2014]` and `[bhattiprolu2025]`
derandomise it by three different routes. Parameterised by the distance bound it
is W[1]-hard to approximate within any constant, over `GF(2)` in
`[bhattacharyya2021]` and over **any fixed finite field** in `[bennett2023]`.

**Neither version is known to reduce to the other, in either direction.** The
hardness proofs are independent, the inapproximability proofs are separated by
twenty-two years, and the obstruction is named: over a finite field one step is
free from the second generalized Hamming weight bound, and over `R` it took a
chaining argument and Littlewood-Offord anticoncentration.

## Two attributions that are routinely got wrong, including here at first

`[berlekamp1978]` proves **coset weight** and **subspace weight** NP-complete, the
second being "is there a codeword of weight *exactly* `w`". It **conjectures**
minimum distance and says so in its own abstract; `[vardy1997]` closed it nineteen
years later. And `[downey1999]` proves *maximum-likelihood decoding* and *weight
distribution* W[1]-hard, **not** minimum distance, which stayed open long enough
to become one of Downey and Fellows' named open questions.

## Why the exact method here does not contradict any of it

[`finite_field_sparsifier.h`](finite_field_sparsifier.h) walks the `q^k` elements
of the column space and takes them in ascending weight. That is exact, by the
matroid greedy, and it is **exponential in `k` and not polynomial in the input**,
so it lives exactly where the hardness does not bite: `k` is 5 or 6 for the
operators here and `q^k` is 32 or 64.

**And it cannot be improved much.** `[stephensdavidowitz2019]` shows that under
SETH there is no `q^((1−ε)n)` algorithm for the minimum distance problem over any
finite field, for a code with `q^n` codewords — where `n` is the dimension, which
is our `k`. So the `q^k` factor is not an artefact of a naive implementation: no
algorithm avoids it, up to the `ε`. Two honest caveats: that is worst-case over
arbitrary codes and says nothing about these structured operators, and it does not
speak to the `2^(0.0951 n)` information-set decoding line, which is about random
codes at fixed rate in the block length rather than the dimension.

## What was read, and what is cited from a citation

**Read**: `[dumer2003]` Theorem 22, `[bhattacharyya2021]` Theorem 6.1 and its dual
formulation, `[cheng2012]` Theorem 1.4, `[stephensdavidowitz2019]`'s abstract
verbatim, `[bennett2023]`'s abstract, `[qu2020]` in full text.

**Not read, and therefore not claimed beyond what cites it**: `[vardy1997]`, whose
abstract IEEE elides everywhere it was sought, so its reduction source is unknown
here; `[downey1999]`, paywalled, its abstract taken from two independent
renderings; `[berlekamp1978]`, abstract verbatim but body only through a
restatement. **Nothing above rests on a paper nobody opened**: where only the
citing literature was available, the sentence says what the citing literature says.

## The gap this repository sits in

`[qu2020]` is the definitive survey of the sparsest-vector problem. It contains
**no occurrence** of "Vardy", "coding theory", "minimum distance", "Berlekamp",
"finite field" or "GF(2)": for hardness it cites `[mccormick1983]` and
`[colemanpothen1986]` and stops. The real-field community and the coding community
are working on one problem and not citing each other, and the finite-field *basis*
problem — ours, the one every operator the rank strand emits poses — falls between
them.

**The same gap costs us on the algorithms side, and there it is sharper.** Coding
theory has been *computing* minimum weights for forty years, and its standard
method Brouwer-Zimmermann `[lisonek2016]` holds a **lower** bound from several
information sets as well as an upper one, so it stops early.
[`method/exact-over-q.md`](method/exact-over-q.md) holds only the upper bound.

**And the exact oracle this module needs is already published, pruned, under
another name.** `[sanjose2025]`'s relative generalized Hamming weight at `r = 1`
is `min{ wt(c) : c ∈ C₁ \ C₂ }`, which is `[beniamini2020]`'s Problem 2.15 with
`C₂` the span of the rows already settled. One community states the problem and
searches column subsets for it; the other has had a Brouwer-Zimmermann-pruned
algorithm for it in ACM TOMS since 2025, shipping in Sage. Neither cites the
other, in either direction. **This file recorded that non-citation on the
hardness side before it noticed the same thing on the algorithms side**, which is
the more expensive half.

One more entry for the hardness table above, over the field this module actually
works in: `[sparsevectorfocs2025]` makes finding the sparsest vector in a **real**
subspace NP-hard to approximate within any constant factor. So the exact method
here is not one theorem away from being polynomial.
